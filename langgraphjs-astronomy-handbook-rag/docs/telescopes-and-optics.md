# Telescopes and Optics

## Cooling down a reflector
A Newtonian or Dobsonian mirror needs time to match the outside air. Set the scope outside 45 minutes before observing, with the dust cover off. Warm mirrors cause tube currents that smear stars into soft blobs.

## Collimation check
Check collimation at the start of every session using the club's Cheshire eyepiece. Adjust the secondary mirror first, then the three primary mirror screws, turning each by no more than a quarter turn at a time. A star that looks like a ring with a dark off-centre hole is out of collimation.

## Choosing an eyepiece
Start with the lowest-power eyepiece, because it gives the widest field and makes objects easy to find. The club's 25 mm eyepiece in a 1200 mm focal length scope gives 48x. Magnification is focal length divided by eyepiece focal length. Never go above about 50x per inch of aperture, and expect to use far less on most nights.

## Using a Barlow lens
A Barlow lens multiplies the power of any eyepiece, so a 2x Barlow turns the 25 mm eyepiece into an effective 12.5 mm. Use it only on the Moon, planets and bright double stars. For faint nebulae it dims the view too much to help.

## Solar observing
Never point a telescope at the Sun without a certified full-aperture solar filter fitted over the front of the tube. Eyepiece solar filters are banned at club events because they can crack from heat. A club safety officer must inspect your filter before the first solar session of the year.
